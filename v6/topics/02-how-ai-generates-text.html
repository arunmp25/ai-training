Example

Same question, two different answers

Suggest a slogan for our home insurance.
“Sleep soundly. We’ve got the roof covered.”
Same prompt, asked again
“Because life has waves.”
It isn’t moody. It chooses likely next words, with a little randomness built in. And it’s billed by the token, a slice of a word: sold by the slice, like pizza 🍕.

Idea 2 · Prediction

An LLM does one thing: predict the next token

Given the text so far, it scores every possible next token. Pick a prompt, then pick a word. Change the temperature and run it 20 times.

⏱ 4 min

🎤 Say An LLM does one thing: it predicts the next token. It is very confident about the capital of France. Ask about your company’s revenue and it still produces a confident number, because producing a likely answer is all it does.

  • Prompt 1 (France): very confident, so the answer is almost always the same.
  • Prompt 3 (revenue) is the killer example: the model has no idea what your revenue is, but it still produces a confident-looking number. That is a hallucination, and now you know why it happens.
  • Temperature: low means it always picks the top word; high means more variety. Run it 20 times to show that the same prompt can give different answers.
Worked example

A confident answer is not a correct answer

Two everyday questions. The model has no facts for either, so it predicts what a right answer usually looks like.

🧾 A policy question
What’s the deductible on policy MTR-55120?
The deductible on MTR-55120 is ₹2,500 for own-damage claims.

❌ Sounds exact, but nothing about this policy was ever in the context. The number is just a likely-looking token sequence.

Fix: give it the facts. Paste the policy schedule, or give the app a get_policy tool (Topic 12), and ask it to quote where the number came from.
💻 A Guidewire question
Which PolicyCenter method gives me a driver’s claim count?
Use driver.getClaimCount() on the PolicyDriver entity.

❌ A plausible Gosu name, so it looks right. But this method doesn’t exist in your configuration, and you only find out when it fails to compile.

Fix: let it read your code. An agent that can search the repo and run ./gwb compile checks its own guess (Topics 15 and 17).
⏱ 2 min

🎤 Say Here is the revenue example in our own world. Both answers are specific, confident and wrong, because the model had nothing to go on and produced what a good answer usually looks like. The fix is never “ask more nicely”; it is to give the model the facts or a way to check.

  • Ask the room: “Has anyone had an AI invent a method name or an API?” Most developers have.
  • The made-up method is the most common hallucination in code. The compile step catches it, which is why agents that run the build are more reliable.
  • ₹2,500 and getClaimCount() are invented for this slide on purpose.
Then repeat

Every answer is built one token at a time

Predict a token, add it to the text, feed it all back in, predict again. Press auto-play.

⏱ 2 min

🎤 Say Every answer is built one token at a time: predict, add, repeat. That is why answers type themselves out on screen, and why the model cannot go back and fix an earlier word.

  • This is why you see answers “type out” on screen: they genuinely are produced word by word.
  • It also means it can’t go back and edit an earlier word. It commits as it goes, which is why asking it to “think step by step first” helps (Topic 7).

Takeaways

What to remember

🧩 Tokens

AI reads and writes in tokens (≈ ¾ word). Price, speed and limits are all counted in tokens.

🎯 Prediction

It predicts the most plausible next token, which is not always the true one. That’s why it hallucinates.

👉 What’s inside?

The engine that does all this is the transformer, and its key idea is attention. That’s the next topic.
