⏱ 3 min

🎤 Say Older models read one word at a time, so by the end of a sentence the beginning had faded. The transformer, from 2017, looks at every word at once. Nothing fades, and because all the words are handled together, it could be trained on enormous amounts of text. That is what made today’s AI possible.

  • First →: the spotlight walks word by word and the words behind it fade. It ends with a weak guess.
  • Second →: every word lights up at once, lines reach back to every word, and “expired” pops in. The thick lines to “policy” and “rejected” are why.
  • If asked: the 2017 paper was called “Attention Is All You Need”. Claude, GPT and Gemini are all transformers.
  • Processing every word together is also why it trains fast on GPUs. No need to go deeper.

Part 1 · The transformer

Inside each layer: meet, then think

Press → to watch the word “policy” pick up its meaning.

1 · Meet2 · Think3 · Repeat
⏱ 3 min

🎤 Say Every layer does two things. First a meeting: each word looks back at the earlier words and takes in what is relevant, which is attention. Then thinking time: each word is processed on its own, using what the model learned in training. Repeat that dozens of times and a plain word like “policy” ends up meaning the customer’s home policy that may not cover flood damage.

  • → Meet: lines reach from “policy” to “insurance”, “home”, “customer” and “storm”, and their colour flows into “policy”.
  • → Think: each word lights up on its own for a moment.
  • → Repeat: the layer counter runs and the meaning of “policy” gets sharper.
  • Words only look back, never forward. That is why the sentence puts “insurance” before “policy”.
  • The rest of this topic zooms into the meet step: attention.

Context changes meaning

“Bank”: river or money?

A word’s meaning is shaped by the words around it. Click “bank” in each sentence.

⏱ 2 min

🎤 Say Same word, different meaning. The words around “bank” decide whether it is a river or money. If your prompt is ambiguous, the model resolves it from whatever context you gave it.

  • This is why context is everything: the same word means different things depending on what surrounds it.
  • Link to prompting: if your prompt is ambiguous, the model resolves it using whatever other words are around, which may not be what you meant.

Why this matters for you · 2

Beginnings and ends get noticed most

Research found models tend to use information at the start and end of a long context best, and can miss things buried in the middle (“lost in the middle”). Move your key instruction.

start of promptmiddleend of prompt
Chance the key detail is used

Illustrative curve. Newer models are much better at long contexts, but the habit still helps: put critical instructions at the start or end, and keep them clear.

⏱ 2 min

🎤 Say Models pay most attention to the start and the end of a long input and can miss details in the middle. So put the task first, the long material in the middle, and repeat the key rule at the end.

  • Practical rule: state the task first, put long material in the middle, and repeat the key constraint at the end.
Worked example

The rule that got lost in the middle

A claims handler asks for a summary of a first notice of loss (FNOL) email. Same facts, same rule, different order.

❌ Before: rule buried mid-way
Hi, can you look at this claim email for me.
From: R. Menon, policy MTR-55120 ...
"I was driving on the ring road on Tuesday
evening when the car in front braked hard..."
Oh, and flag it if the car was being used for
paid deliveries, because that's excluded.
"...I'd just dropped off the last food order
and was heading home when..."  (30 more lines)
"...the bumper and left headlamp are damaged."
Thanks!
Summary: Rear-end collision on the ring road, Tuesday evening. Bumper and headlamp damage. No injuries. (No delivery flag.)
✅ After: task first, data tagged, rule repeated
Summarise this FNOL email for a motor claims
handler in 5 bullets. Flag any sign the car was
used for paid deliveries (excluded cover).

<email>
From: R. Menon, policy MTR-55120 ...  (70 lines)
</email>

Remember: say "DELIVERY USE: yes / no / unclear"
as the last bullet, quoting the line you used.
Summary: … • DELIVERY USE: unclear. Driver says “I’d just dropped off the last food order”. Check with the customer before deciding cover.
⏱ 3 min

🎤 Say This is the lost-in-the-middle effect on a real task. On the left the one rule that matters is buried between two long chunks of email, and the summary misses it. On the right the task comes first, the email is wrapped in tags, and the rule is repeated at the end, so the important line is caught.

  • The “before” is how most people actually write: chatty opening, paste, then an afterthought.
  • Notice the “after” also asks for a quote. That makes the answer easy to check (more in Topic 7).
  • Outputs are illustrative. Newer models often catch the buried rule, but the habit makes it far more reliable.

Takeaways

What to remember

🏗️ Transformer

An assembly line that sees every word at once: tokens, numbers, dozens of layers that meet and think, then a guess.

🔦 Attention

Every word looks at every other word to work out what it means here.

🧭 Context shapes meaning

Ambiguity gets resolved by whatever is around it. Be explicit.

🎯 Focus wins

Less noise, clear structure, key instructions at the start or end.
