Embeddings in practice: search by meaning

An embedding turns any text into a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so a computer can find things by what they mean, not the words they use. Where it helps, what it costs, and how to choose a model.

⏱ 30 sec

🎤 Say RAG needs to find the right pages by meaning, not by matching words. Embeddings are how. Today is about where they help, what they cost and how to choose one; the maths is in an optional deep dive at the end.

  • Promise: by the end everyone can explain “embedding” in one sentence and name three jobs it does at an insurer.

Practical uses

Six everyday jobs for embeddings

The same trick every time: embed the things, embed the question, take the nearest. Press → for the next job, or click one.

⏱ 4 min

🎤 Say RAG is only one use. Anything you can describe in words can be embedded and compared: policy clauses, emails, claims, FAQs, IT tickets. Embed them once, embed the new item, and take the nearest ones.

  • Each → shows the next job: the dots are the stored items, the yellow chip is the new question, the green lines are the nearest matches.
  • Routing emails: instead of writing rules, keep a few example emails per team. New examples improve it without code.
  • Duplicates: a very high score (0.97) is a signal for a person to check, never an automatic rejection.
  • FAQs: when the stored item is an approved answer, you can show it as is. No text generation, so no hallucination.

🎤 Say Every chunk gets a score. Top-k decides how many to keep and the threshold decides the minimum score. Too many adds noise, too few misses part of the answer.

  • k too high: noise and cost (and attention dilution, Topic 4). k too low: you miss the second half of the answer.
  • Scores are relative, not percentages of truth. 0.78 is “good” for one model and “meh” for another, so tune thresholds on your own data.

⏱ 2 min

🎤 Say Embedding the whole 20,000-page manual costs about a dollar and takes minutes. Answering a thousand questions a day costs hundreds of dollars a month. So search is cheap; the model reading and writing is the cost to watch.

  • Start at 20,000 pages (the manual from Topic 7). Then drag to 100,000: the embedding cost is still a few dollars.
  • The monthly answer cost does not depend on library size: each question still sends about five chunks.
  • Switching embedding model means re-embedding everything. Cheap in money, but plan the time and testing.
  • Self-hosted open models cost no per-token fee, but you pay for the servers.

Takeaways

What to remember

📍 Embedding = meaning as numbers

A model turns text into a list of numbers. Similar meaning, similar numbers, so search works without shared words.

🧰 Embed once, find the nearest

Search, routing emails, similar claims, duplicates, FAQs, tickets. Embedding is cheap; answering is the cost.

Same model for documents and questions. Shortlist two or three, test on your own questions, tune top-k.

Facts worth knowing
• Typical sizes: 384, 768, 1,024, 1,536 or 3,072 dimensions.
• Same input + same model = same vector, every time.

Deep dive · Measuring closeness

Cosine similarity: do two arrows point the same way?

Think of each vector as an arrow from the origin. Cosine similarity looks only at the angle between them: same direction = 1, unrelated (90°) = 0, opposite = −1. Drag the arrow tips.

Cosine similarity
⏱ 4 min

🎤 Say Cosine similarity asks one question: do two arrows point the same way? It ignores length, which suits text of different lengths.

  • Try “Same direction, longer”: cosine stays 1.00 even though B is twice as long, while the distance changes. Cosine ignores length, which is why it suits text: a long paragraph and a short sentence about the same thing should still match.
  • Real embeddings have 1,536 dimensions, not 2, but the maths and the idea are identical.
Deep dive · Worked example

Calculate cosine similarity yourself

cos(A, B) = (A · B) ÷ (|A| × |B|). Multiply matching numbers and add them up (the dot product), then divide by the two lengths. Edit any number.

⏱ 3 min

🎤 Say Here is the calculation by hand: multiply matching numbers, add them up, divide by the lengths. Change dog into a truck and watch the score drop.

  • Dimensions: [animal, pet, vehicle]. Walk the “puppy vs dog” row: 0.9×0.8 + 0.8×0.9 + 0×0.1 = 1.44, lengths 1.20 and 1.21, so cos ≈ 0.99.
  • Ask someone to change “dog” into a truck (0, 0, 0.9) and watch the score collapse.
  • A vector database does exactly this, just with 1,536 numbers and millions of chunks, very fast.
